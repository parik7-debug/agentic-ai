What is scarcity?	The fundamental economic problem that arises because resources are limited but human wants are unlimited. | Tip: Scarcity forces every individual, firm, and government to make choices. It's the foundation of all economic reasoning.
What is economics?	The study of how individuals and societies allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants. | Example: Deciding whether to use land for farming or housing is an economic decision driven by scarcity.
What is opportunity cost?	The value of the next best alternative foregone when a choice is made. | Example: If you spend $1,000 on a vacation instead of investing it, the opportunity cost is the return you could have earned.
What is a trade-off?	Giving up one thing to get another; every decision involves trade-offs because of scarcity. | Tip: Trade-offs differ from opportunity cost — opportunity cost is specifically the BEST forgone alternative, not all alternatives.
What are the factors of production?	Land, Labor, Capital, and Entrepreneurship — the four resources used to produce goods and services. | Tip: Remember with the acronym LLCE. Capital here means physical capital (machines, tools), not money.
What is the difference between productive efficiency and allocative efficiency?	Productive efficiency = producing at lowest possible cost. Allocative efficiency = producing the mix of goods most valued by society. | Tip: A PPC shows productive efficiency at any point ON the curve; allocative efficiency is the specific point that best satisfies society's wants.
What is a Production Possibilities Curve (PPC)?	A graph showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all resources are fully and efficiently employed. | Example: A PPC showing guns vs. butter illustrates the trade-off a nation faces when allocating resources between defense and food.
What does a point inside the PPC represent?	Productive inefficiency — resources are unemployed or used inefficiently. | Example: A high unemployment rate would put an economy inside its PPC.
What does a point outside the PPC represent?	Currently unattainable — the economy lacks the resources or technology to produce that combination. | Tip: A future point outside today's PPC becomes achievable through economic growth (technology or resource increase).
What causes the PPC to shift outward?	Economic growth: an increase in the quantity or quality of resources or an improvement in technology. | Example: A breakthrough in solar panel technology shifts the PPC outward, especially on the energy-goods axis.
Why is the PPC typically bowed outward (concave)?	Because of the law of increasing opportunity costs — resources are not perfectly adaptable between uses. | Example: To produce more butter, you reassign workers progressively better suited for gun production, making each unit of butter more costly.
What is a linear PPC and what does it imply?	A straight-line PPC implies a constant opportunity cost — resources are perfectly substitutable between the two goods. | Example: If every worker is equally good at making apples or oranges, the PPC is linear.
What is absolute advantage?	The ability of a producer to produce more of a good than another producer using the same amount of resources. | Example: Country A makes 100 cars/hour while Country B makes 60. Country A has absolute advantage in cars.
What is comparative advantage?	The ability of a producer to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer. | Tip: Comparative advantage — NOT absolute advantage — is what drives specialization and gains from trade.
How do you calculate comparative advantage?	Compare opportunity costs: the producer with the lower opportunity cost in a good has the comparative advantage in that good. | Example: If Country A gives up 2 wheat per car and Country B gives up 3 wheat per car, Country A has comparative advantage in cars.
What is the principle of specialization?	Producers should specialize in the good in which they have a comparative advantage and trade for other goods. | Tip: Specialization and trade allow both parties to consume outside their individual PPCs.
What are terms of trade?	The exchange rate agreed upon between trading partners; must fall between the two producers' opportunity costs for trade to benefit both. | Example: If Country A's OC is 2 wheat/car and Country B's is 3 wheat/car, a terms of trade of 2.5 wheat/car benefits both.
What is a market economy?	An economic system in which decisions about production and consumption are made by individuals through the price system with little government intervention. | Example: The United States operates largely as a market economy where supply and demand set prices.
What is a command (planned) economy?	An economic system in which the government makes all major economic decisions about production and distribution. | Example: The former Soviet Union is a classic example of a command economy.
What is a mixed economy?	An economy that combines elements of both market and command economies; most real-world economies are mixed. | Example: Most modern nations, including the U.S., have mixed economies with some government regulation and some free markets.
What are the three fundamental economic questions every society must answer?	What to produce? How to produce it? For whom to produce it? | Tip: These questions arise because of scarcity. Different economic systems answer them differently — markets vs. governments.
What is a positive economic statement?	A factual, testable statement about the way things are — describes economic reality without value judgments. | Example: 'The unemployment rate is 4%' is a positive statement.
What is a normative economic statement?	A value-based statement about how things ought to be — reflects opinions and cannot be tested empirically. | Example: 'The government should raise the minimum wage' is a normative statement.
What is the difference between microeconomics and macroeconomics?	Microeconomics studies individual decision-makers (consumers/firms/markets); macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole (GDP/inflation/unemployment). | Tip: Micro = small-scale (tree); Macro = big-scale (forest). AP Micro focuses on individual markets and resource allocation.
What is utility?	The satisfaction or benefit a consumer receives from consuming a good or service. | Tip: Utility is subjective — different consumers receive different utility from the same product.
What is the ceteris paribus assumption?	Latin for 'all else equal' — holding all other variables constant when analyzing the effect of one variable. | Example: To study how price affects quantity demanded, we hold income, tastes, and prices of related goods constant.
What is the role of the entrepreneur in the economy?	Entrepreneurs combine land, labor, and capital to produce goods and services; they bear risk and are rewarded with profit. | Example: A startup founder who organizes resources and assumes financial risk to launch a new app is acting as an entrepreneur.
